Addressing the stagnation of reading outcomes in Northeastern Nigeria: A critical evaluation of international development interventions.

What explains the persistent gap between education programming and foundational reading outcomes — and what can be done to close it?

By Lazarus Samson Yunana · MERLA Specialist

Abstract

This paper explores the stagnation in reading outcomes in Northeastern Nigeria despite over a decade of international development interventions aimed at improving literacy. Drawing on Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA) findings and related education programmes, it considers the emphasis on outputs rather than learning outcomes, gaps in educators' capacity to administer and use assessments, and wider cultural and policy barriers. It proposes stronger diagnostic assessment, teacher development and outcome-oriented policy as part of a collaborative approach to improving foundational literacy.

1. Introduction

Northeastern Nigeria continues to face significant educational challenges. Numerous international organisations and government programmes have sought to improve access to primary education through increased enrolment, learning materials and teacher training. However, persistent weaknesses in foundational reading outcomes raise questions about whether improvements in programme delivery are translating into meaningful learning gains.

This article examines the gap between education outputs — including enrolment and infrastructure — and actual learning outcomes. It considers why foundational literacy remains a challenge and what a more evidence-informed approach could look like.

2. Background and literature

2.1 Education interventions in Nigeria

Programmes such as USAID's Northern Education Initiative Plus (NEI+) and UNICEF's Girls' Education Project (GEP3) have sought to improve education access and quality in Northern Nigeria. Their activities have included enrolment support, learning materials and teacher development. However, improvements in access do not automatically demonstrate improvements in literacy.

2.2 The role of EGRA

The Early Grade Reading Assessment is a diagnostic tool for examining early-grade reading skills, including fluency and comprehension. It can help identify learning gaps that are not visible through administrative measures such as enrolment, attendance or grade progression.

2.3 Poor proxies for learning

Enrolment, attendance, classroom participation and infrastructure are useful indicators of access and programme implementation. They are not direct measures of whether a child can read and understand a text. Treating them as substitutes for learning outcomes can create an incomplete picture of progress.

3. Findings

3.1 Stagnation in reading outcomes

The source manuscript reports that EGRA data from 2013 to 2023 showed limited improvement in oral reading fluency and comprehension in the settings reviewed. For Adamawa State in 2023, it cites an average oral reading fluency score of 1.18 correct words per minute, with 87% of pupils scoring zero, and reports that 93.7% could not answer any comprehension questions correctly. These figures are reproduced as reported in the supplied manuscript and require checking against the original assessment report before external use.

3.2 A gap between teacher perceptions and pupil performance

The manuscript reports that 96% of teachers said they conducted regular assessments, while describing many of these practices as focused on classroom participation or routine exercises rather than direct assessment of reading skills. This points to the importance of strengthening teachers' understanding of what assessment evidence can and cannot demonstrate.

3.3 Reliance on inadequate proxies

Attendance, classroom behaviour and grade promotion can indicate participation and progression, but they may mask gaps in reading fluency and comprehension. Programme monitoring should therefore pair implementation indicators with direct measures of learning.

4. Discussion

4.1 Outputs rather than outcomes

Enrolment, classrooms and learning materials matter, especially in underserved communities. Yet these outputs should not be treated as proof of improved literacy. Monitoring frameworks need to follow the chain from inputs and activities through to learning outcomes.

4.2 Capacity to assess and respond

Assessment tools are useful only when educators can administer them appropriately, interpret the results and adapt instruction. Capacity development should include practical use of assessment findings, not only training on tool administration.

4.3 Cultural and systemic barriers

Socio-cultural expectations, gender inequality, insecurity, poverty, language differences and institutional constraints can affect school participation and learning. These factors require context-sensitive responses developed with educators, communities and public institutions.

5. Recommendations

5.1 Scale up diagnostic assessment

  • Use EGRA and complementary diagnostic tools appropriately and consistently.
  • Build education administrators' and teachers' capacity to interpret results.
  • Use assessment evidence to identify specific learning gaps and guide support.

5.2 Strengthen teacher assessment practice

  • Train teachers in formative and summative assessment approaches.
  • Support teachers to adapt instruction to learners' demonstrated needs.
  • Provide ongoing professional development and locally relevant materials.

5.3 Prioritise outcome-based evaluation

  • Include measurable literacy outcomes in education policies and programme frameworks.
  • Use direct learning evidence alongside access and participation indicators.
  • Establish feedback loops that connect assessment findings to resource allocation and programme adaptation.

5.4 Build sustainable collaboration

  • Strengthen collaboration among government, donors, implementing organisations, educators and communities.
  • Support local institutions to sustain assessment and teacher development systems.
  • Ensure resources address learning quality as well as access to education.

6. Conclusion

Persistent challenges in foundational literacy cannot be understood through enrolment and attendance figures alone. Education interventions need to look beyond outputs and examine whether children are developing the skills they need to read and comprehend. Strengthening diagnostic assessment, teacher capacity, context-sensitive programming and outcome-focused policy can help ensure that investments in education translate into measurable and lasting learning improvements.

Editorial and evidence note: This article is based on the text supplied by the author. The reported statistics, descriptions of named programmes and comparative claims have not been independently verified here. The supplied material did not include a complete, traceable bibliography or sufficient methodological detail to validate all figures. Verify the original EGRA reports, sample, grade, language, assessment period and references before citing this as a formal research paper.
