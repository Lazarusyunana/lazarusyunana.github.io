Challenging poor proxies to learning: A decade of stagnant reading outcomes in Northeastern Nigeria.

Why enrolment, attendance and grade progression cannot tell us whether children are actually learning to read.

By Lazarus Samson Yunana · MERLA Specialist · May 2025

For more than a decade, education interventions in Northeastern Nigeria have sought to improve access to schooling and strengthen foundational learning. Yet the evidence presented in this paper raises an important question: are programmes measuring what matters most — whether children are learning?

The problem with measuring progress indirectly

Attendance, enrolment, classroom participation and grade promotion are important programme indicators. They tell us something about access, participation and progression. But they are not direct measures of reading proficiency. A child can attend school regularly, complete exercises and move to the next grade without mastering the foundational skills needed to read and understand a text.

When these indicators become stand-ins for learning, programmes risk reporting progress without knowing whether learning has improved. The challenge is not that output indicators have no value; it is that they cannot, on their own, demonstrate changes in learning outcomes.

What direct assessment can reveal

The Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA) is designed to assess foundational reading skills, including letter-sound knowledge, decoding, oral reading fluency and comprehension. Used appropriately, it can help identify specific learning gaps and inform instructional responses.

The analysis underpinning this paper draws attention to very low reading performance reported in assessments in Northeastern Nigeria, including Adamawa State. The figures cited in the original manuscript — including an average oral reading fluency of 1.18 correct words per minute and a high proportion of pupils scoring zero — should be read alongside the original assessment reports and their sampling, language, grade and administration details.

Why interventions may not translate into learning gains

1. Outputs can overshadow outcomes

Education programmes often have strong incentives to track visible outputs: enrolment, materials distributed, teachers trained and classrooms supported. These are necessary measures of implementation, but they do not establish whether learners have acquired foundational skills. Monitoring frameworks need to connect delivery indicators to direct measures of learning.

2. Assessment capacity matters

Diagnostic tools only improve teaching when educators and administrators can administer them appropriately, interpret the results and act on what they find. Training should therefore go beyond tool administration to include analysis, feedback and practical adaptation of instruction.

3. Context shapes what is possible

Conflict, displacement, poverty, language diversity, social expectations and uneven access to schools all influence learning. Assessment findings must be interpreted in context, and interventions should be designed with communities, educators and local institutions rather than assuming that one model will work everywhere.

Moving from proxy indicators to evidence of learning

A stronger approach does not discard access and participation indicators. It places them alongside direct measures of learning and uses each indicator for the question it can answer. Programmes can strengthen this approach by:

  • Using EGRA and complementary diagnostic assessments at appropriate intervals and with clear quality standards.
  • Building teachers' and education officers' capacity to interpret results and adapt instruction.
  • Including direct learning measures in routine monitoring and programme reviews.
  • Disaggregating results appropriately and interpreting them in light of language, grade, location and learner characteristics.
  • Creating feedback loops so evidence informs classroom practice, programme adaptation and policy dialogue.
  • Working with government, schools, communities and development partners to sustain assessment and improvement systems.

Conclusion

Improving access to education remains essential, but access is not the same as learning. If programmes want to understand whether children are acquiring foundational reading skills, they must measure those skills directly and support educators to use the evidence. Moving beyond poor proxies is not simply a technical adjustment; it is a commitment to making programme success reflect the learning experiences and outcomes of children.
